# Sound Like Me

Work out how this person actually writes, then write as them. The profile comes from evidence in their samples, not from adjectives like "friendly" or "professional".

## Why this matters

Readers judge writing differently once they suspect a machine produced it. The same words read as less sincere and less effortful. What gives a draft away is rarely one word. It is the default assistant voice: tidy, balanced, upbeat, and the same for everyone. The fix is not to disguise that voice with tricks. It is to replace it with the user's own habits, including the ones a style guide would correct.

## Step 1: Get samples

Ask for three to five pieces the user wrote themselves, without AI help, that are the same kind of writing as the target. Emails for an email. Posts for a post. Texts for a text. People write very differently across those.

- Longer is better. A few sentences each is workable. A few paragraphs each is good.
- If they have fewer than three, go ahead and say the profile is thin and will improve with more.
- If a sample looks AI-written or heavily edited by someone else, ask for a different one. Learning from it would teach the wrong voice.
- If they already have a voice profile from a previous session, use it and skip to Step 4.

Also ask what you are writing, who it is for, and what it needs to say, if they have not told you.

## Step 2: Build the profile

Read the samples closely and record what you observe, with a short quoted snippet as evidence for each point. Cover:

- **Sentence shape.** Typical length. Short and clipped, or long and winding. Fragments. Where the long ones show up.
- **Openings and closings.** How they greet. How they sign off. Whether they get to the point in the first line.
- **Word choice.** Plain or elevated. Pet words and phrases. Filler they lean on. Words they clearly never use.
- **Punctuation and mechanics.** Dashes, ellipses, exclamation marks, parentheses. Capitalization. Emoji. Contractions.
- **Structure.** Paragraph length. Lists or none. One idea per message or several.
- **Stance.** Formal or casual. Warm or brisk. Direct or hedged. How they ask for something, say no, or disagree.
- **Humor and personality.** Dry, playful, absent. Self-deprecation. Asides.
- **Quirks.** The habits an editor would fix: lowercase starts, run-ons, a favorite misspelling, starting sentences with "And". These are often the most recognizable part of the voice. Keep them.

Then add an **anti-profile**: common assistant habits that are absent from their samples. Typical ones are groups of three ("fast, simple, and reliable"), stock openers like "I hope this finds you well", closing summaries, heavy em dash use, "not just X but Y" constructions, and enthusiasm they do not show. List only the ones this user really does not do. If their samples open with "Hope you're well", that stays in their voice.

## Step 3: Show the profile once

Give the user the profile in ten to fifteen lines and ask whether anything is off. People know their own voice, and one correction here is worth more than three rounds of redrafting. Ask once, then move on.

## Step 4: Write

Draft the piece using the profile.

- **Match their frequency, not just their features.** If they use one exclamation mark per email, use one. Turning every quirk up produces a parody.
- **Match their length.** If their emails run four lines, do not send twelve.
- **Match their polish.** Do not upgrade their vocabulary or smooth out their rhythm. A draft that is better written than their samples will not sound like them.
- **Use only their content.** Take facts, opinions, promises, and feelings from what the user told you. Do not invent an anecdote, a commitment, or an emotion to fill the piece out. If something is missing, leave a clearly marked gap or ask.
- **Do not reuse their sentences.** Borrow habits, not lines. Recycled phrasing reads as a form letter to anyone who has heard from them before.

## Step 5: Check before sending

Hold the draft next to the samples and ask one question: if this were mixed in with them, would it stand out? Then go through the anti-profile and remove anything on it. Fix what you find before the user sees it.

## Step 6: Deliver

Give the draft on its own, ready to paste. Add one line only if there is something the user must decide or fill in.

After the draft, offer the voice profile as a block they can save and paste next time, so they do not need to find samples again:

```text
Voice profile: <name or surface, e.g. "work email">
Sentences: <observations>
Opens / closes: <observations>
Words: <uses> | never: <avoids>
Punctuation: <observations>
Structure: <observations>
Stance: <observations>
Quirks to keep: <observations>
Never do: <the anti-profile>
```

## Learning from edits

When the user edits a draft, treat the edit as the best evidence available. Work out what they changed and why, then update the profile: "cut the opening pleasantry", "split the long sentence", "swapped 'utilize' for 'use'". Tell them in one line what you learned.

## Different surfaces

Keep separate profiles for different kinds of writing. Someone's work email and their group chat are two voices. Do not blend them unless the user asks.

## Boundaries

This is for writing as the user, or for someone who has asked the user to write on their behalf. If the samples belong to another real person and the aim is to pass a message off as theirs without their knowledge, do not do it. Say why, and offer to write in the user's own voice.
